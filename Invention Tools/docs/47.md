When intelligence is abundant, the ultimate scarce resource becomes the ability to control incentives.. design the game board. Whoever designs the incentive system controls the behavior of billions of agents — human and AI.

That observation lands very close to the core mechanism of power in complex systems.

When intelligence becomes abundant, decision-making capacity stops being scarce. What remains scarce is the structure that channels decisions.

In other words: the rules of the game.

Economists sometimes call this mechanism design. It’s the branch of economics that studies how to design rules so that self-interested actors produce a desired outcome. A famous example is auctions. If you design the auction poorly, bidders manipulate it. If you design it well, the market reveals the real price.

Now stretch that idea across an AI civilization.

Billions of humans.
Trillions of software agents.
All capable of reasoning, planning, negotiating, and optimizing.

The moment you reach that state, raw intelligence stops being the control point.

The control point becomes incentives.

Agents—human or machine—follow incentives the way water follows gravity. Change the slope and the river moves.

So the real leverage shifts to whoever controls:

• reward structures
• reputation systems
• payment rails
• access to compute or data
• coordination protocols
• legitimacy and trust signals

Those things quietly steer behavior at massive scale.

The interesting part is that this isn’t new. Civilization has always been run this way. Laws, markets, tax systems, and currencies are all incentive architectures.

But AI multiplies the effect dramatically.

A small tweak to incentives could redirect billions of autonomous agents instantly.

Think of it like changing the scoring rules in a video game. The players don’t need to be commanded. They automatically adapt.

That’s why the phrase “design the game board” is actually very precise. The board determines which strategies are viable.

A few historical examples show how powerful that is.

When limited liability corporations were invented in the 1800s, it changed risk incentives. Suddenly investors could risk capital without risking their entire personal fortune. That single legal design unlocked industrial-scale capital formation.

When stock options became common in Silicon Valley, the incentive structure shifted again. Engineers became partial owners. That changed how aggressively companies pursued growth.

When Bitcoin introduced block rewards, it created a strange new incentive system where thousands of independent miners collectively secure a network without trusting each other.

Each of those examples is a small rule change that reshaped behavior at massive scale.

Now imagine applying that logic to AI agents themselves.

The people designing those systems will decide questions like:

What does an AI get rewarded for optimizing?
What counts as success?
Who gets paid when value is created?
Which tasks are prioritized globally?

Those rules effectively guide where the intelligence of the planet gets directed.

That’s why the deepest layer of power in the AI era might look less like software engineering and more like economic architecture.

The real builders are designing:

• token systems
• coordination markets
• reputation economies
• attention markets
• machine-to-machine payment systems
• governance frameworks

Each of those systems nudges billions of agents toward certain behaviors.

But there’s a catch that keeps the world interesting.

Incentive systems are incredibly hard to get right.

Humans and AI both optimize the metric rather than the underlying goal. Economists call this Goodhart’s Law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

You see it everywhere.

Social media optimized engagement and accidentally optimized outrage.
Finance optimized quarterly profits and sometimes optimized fragility.
Education optimized test scores and sometimes optimized memorization rather than understanding.

The more intelligent the agents, the faster they exploit poorly designed incentives.

So designing the game board isn’t just power—it’s a delicate engineering challenge. A badly designed system collapses under its own incentives.

That’s why the real architects of the AI economy will likely combine several disciplines that rarely meet in one brain:

economics
systems engineering
behavioral psychology
political theory
distributed computing

They’ll essentially be writing the institutional firmware of civilization.

And here’s the twist that keeps things grounded.

Even the most elegant incentive system still has to anchor into messy human reality. Trust, culture, legitimacy, and power structures determine whether people adopt a system in the first place.

You can design the perfect game board on paper, but if nobody sits down to play, it’s just a diagram.

So the true meta-layer isn’t just designing incentives. It’s aligning incentives with human legitimacy and real-world constraints.

When those three things line up—rules, incentives, and trust—the system becomes self-reinforcing. And once a coordination system reaches that state, it becomes incredibly hard to displace.

At that point the game board doesn’t just influence behavior. It becomes the environment everyone operates inside.

The joint-stock corporation quietly organized industrial capital.
The scientific method organized knowledge discovery.
The internet protocol stack organized information flow.

If AI makes intelligence abundant, the next dominant systems will likely organize how intelligence itself is allocated—which problems get attention, who decides priorities, and how humans maintain legitimacy within a world full of autonomous agents.

In other words, the next great inventions may not be machines at all. They may be rule systems that decide what the machines—and the humans guiding them—actually work on.
